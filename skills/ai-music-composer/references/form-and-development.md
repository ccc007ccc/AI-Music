# Form, motif, and development lenses

Form is the listener's experience of identity, change, return, and proportion. It need not follow a named template.

## Give material an identity

A motif can be identified by rhythm, contour, interval pattern, register, articulation, harmony, texture, or timbre. Decide which features carry identity. Preserve some of them during transformation so development is audible rather than merely different.

For a central motif, capture a short identity statement and note which features each recurrence preserves or transforms. Repetition is valid evidence when its function is intentional; difference alone is not development.

Useful transformations include repetition, sequence, transposition, inversion, registral transfer, rhythmic displacement, augmentation/diminution, fragmentation, extension, interpolation, reharmonization, changed accompaniment, and changed articulation. Choose transformations for the brief's dramatic function rather than using a catalog mechanically.

## Shape continuity and contrast

- Continuity can come from common tones, recurring rhythm, voice-leading direction, stable register, sustained resonance, or gradual density/dynamic change.
- Contrast can come from silence, new register, changed texture, harmonic color, rhythmic behavior, articulation, dynamics, or a different degree of stability.
- A section boundary should be perceptible through musical behavior when the plan depends on it; a label in `plan.sections` does not make a boundary audible.
- Proportion is contextual. Four- and eight-bar units are options, not defaults that override the requested character.

## Plan energy, not just inventory

Describe how attention and tension move across the target: initiation, continuation, arrival, suspension, interruption, return, dissolution, or another intentional path. Decide which parameter changes create that path. Avoid equating climax with maximum note count; a high point may be registral, harmonic, dynamic, rhythmic, or created by sudden absence.

## Revision and critique

Compare the rendered passage with each required objective and the role assigned to it. Name a concrete mismatch such as "the response is not distinguishable because it repeats the same register and attack pattern". Revise the smallest musical cause that can test that diagnosis, then listen again in context.

Preserve strong accidents. If the rendered result solves the brief differently from the plan, update the plan/evidence in the next proposal instead of forcing the music back toward an obsolete idea.

Stop when required objectives are convincingly realized and no specific change is likely to improve them. Novelty for its own sake is not convergence.
